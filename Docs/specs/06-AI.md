# 06 — AI System Specification

Source: `Core/AISystem.cs`, `Data/AIState.cs`. GDD §24.

## 1. Principles

1. **The AI plays the same game.** Wherever a verb has an actor-generic
   `*By(actorId)` form, the AI acts through it. It does not have private
   mechanics for anything the player can also do.
2. **The AI is under the same fog.** It reasons from `IntelligenceSystem`
   estimates, never truth, and can be deceived — and, since the deception verb
   became actor-generic, it can deceive.
3. **No hidden stat cheats.** Difficulty changes reasoning quality only.
   `AISystemTests.Difficulty_ChangesReasoningQualityNotStats` asserts Standard
   and Ruthless worlds start with identical capabilities.
4. **Governments are not identical optimizers.** Personality spread and scoring
   noise keep behavior varied.

`AISystem.MonthlyThink` is wired into `ResolveMonth` in `GameController.Attach`,
after the pillar systems and before `ConfrontationSystem.MonthlyTick` — so a
government reasons on a world the other systems have already updated this month,
and any confrontation it opens or escalates ticks in the same month. Each
government reasons under its own deterministic RNG:

```
seed = rngSeed × 6700417 + monthIndex × 733 + Hash.Of(countryId)
```

Per government, in order: `UpdatePlayerAssessment` → `FormObjectives` →
`TrackRivalries` → `Act` → `ManageOngoingConfrontation` →
`ConsiderStrategicInstruments`.

`SeedAI` creates an `AIState` for every **non-player** country at world creation.
A country the player is not posted to always has one; the player's own never
does.

## 2. Personality

`AIProfile` per country, each axis rolled 20–80 (one decimal) at world creation:
`aggression`, `caution`, `opportunism`, `patience`.

- `aggression` → coercion appetite, escalation chance, operation tempo,
  willingness to reach for a strategic instrument
- `caution` → discounts conclusions drawn from thin reporting; restrains
  collateral risk; prompts earlier settlement when losing
- `opportunism` → weight on exploiting perceived weakness; size of a deception
  programme
- `patience` → objective review interval and tolerance for exhaustion before
  seeking terms

## 3. Difficulty (GDD §24.3)

| Difficulty | Actions/month | Planning horizon | Objectives kept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 1 | 0.45 | 1 |
| Challenging | 2 | 0.70 | 2 |
| Ruthless | 3 | 1.00 | 3 |

Objectives kept **is** the action budget. It used to be capped at 2 while Ruthless
was granted 3 actions, so the hardest difficulty's third action point was
unreachable through `Act` — the extra reasoning it was supposed to buy did not
exist. Test: `AIStrategyTests.TheHardestDifficultyCanUseAllOfItsReasoning`.

Horizon weights long-payoff objectives (`ExpandInfluence`, `AssertClaim`) and
raises the monthly chance of committing to a strategic programme. Ruthless
additionally adds +0.05 to claim commitment chance.

**The budget only governs objective execution.** `actionsThisMonth` is reset and
spent inside `Act`; `ManageOngoingConfrontation` and
`ConsiderStrategicInstruments` run every month for every government regardless of
difficulty. A Standard-difficulty state still prosecutes its war properly — it
simply does one thing a month outside of it.

## 4. Objective scoring

Objectives age one month each month. The list is re-scored when the **first**
objective's `monthsPursued` is a multiple of the review interval:

```
reviewInterval = 3 + floor(patience / 20)      → 4–7 months at rolled personalities
```

Candidates:

| Objective | Trigger | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| `ConsolidateHome` | stability < 50 or approval < 40 | `(60−stability) + (50−approval)×0.6` |
| `SecureResources` | energy < 50 or materials < 45 | `(55 − min(energy, materials)) × 1.1` |
| `PreemptProgramme` | `detectedProgramme > 15` | `60 + detectedProgramme × 1.6` |
| `CounterRival` | `threat − cautionPenalty > 35` and a response remains | `(threat − cautionPenalty) × (0.7 + aggression/200)` |
| `AssertClaim` | perceived weakness > 8 and relations < 50 | see below |
| `ExpandInfluence` | relations > 55 and no treaty | `relations × 0.5 × (0.6 + horizon×0.6)` |
| `BuildCapability` | always (fallback) | `30 + random(0,12)` |

`PreemptProgramme` is **appended** to `AIObjectiveType`, never inserted:
`JsonUtility` persists an enum by ordinal, so inserting it above an existing
member would silently reinterpret every saved objective in every save on disk.

```
threat = theirThreatPerception + max(0, perceivedStrength − ourMilitary) × 0.6
       + (50 − relations) × 0.4
       + detectedProgramme          ← §4b, spec 14 §6; also gates §5b
cautionPenalty = (1 − confidenceFactor) × caution × 0.5

perceivedWeakness = max(0, ourMilitary − perceivedStrength)
assertClaim = ( perceivedWeakness × 1.5 × (opportunism/100) × (0.6 + horizon×0.8)
              × (0.5 + 0.5 × confidenceFactor)
              + (40 − relations) × 0.35 )  ← hostility itself invites pressure
            × reach                        ← GeographySystem.ReachFactorTo, 0.2..1
```

**`reach` multiplies the whole claim score, `CounterRival` is not weighted at
all** (spec 01 §3b). A government does not press a claim it has no way to
prosecute, so a weak *neighbour* is a far more attractive target than an equally
weak state on the other side of the world; without this, regional powers picked
fights across the planet and geography was invisible in how the world behaved.
Alarm is treated differently from ambition on purpose: a distant threat is still
a threat, and the answer to one you cannot reach is sanctions, alignment and
collection — all of which `CounterRival` can already choose. Test:
`GeographySystemTests.AmbitionIsBoundedByReach`.

The rival loop runs once per other country, so several `CounterRival`,
`AssertClaim` and `ExpandInfluence` candidates can be generated in one review.
Every candidate then receives ±12 random noise so states do not converge, and the
top `keep` are retained — **replacing** the previous list entirely.

`confidenceFactor`: 1.0 Confirmed / 0.85 High / 0.65 Moderate / 0.45 Low /
0.35 none. Note the claim formula uses `0.5 + 0.5 × factor` — bad reporting makes
a government **hesitant, not paralyzed**. An earlier build multiplied by the raw
factor and the world became implausibly peaceful.

`PerceivedStrength` reads `IntelligenceSystem.GetEstimate`; with no usable
estimate it falls back to `40 + marketIndex × 0.1`, which is public information.
The AI never touches a foreign country's true pillar value.

### 4a. Rivalry, which outlives objectives

Because the objective list is **replaced** at every review, `monthsPursued`
structurally cannot exceed the review interval (4–7 months). Anything gated on a
long-standing rivalry — strategic preparation needs 24 months — could therefore
*never fire* while reading objective age. `AIState.rivalries` tracks the slower
fact underneath.

`TrackRivalries` runs immediately after `FormObjectives`, on the fresh list:

- Every `CounterRival` **or `PreemptProgramme`** objective naming a state adds a
  month to that state's `Rivalry.months` and clears its `coolOff`.
- A rivalry not named this month accumulates `coolOff`; at **three** quiet months
  it sheds one month of enmity and resets the counter. At zero months the rivalry
  is removed.

Counting `PreemptProgramme` is not a nicety. Pre-empting a state is the strongest
possible way of treating it as a rival, and because the new objective *displaces*
`CounterRival` whenever it scores higher — which it does by a wide margin (§5b) —
reading only `CounterRival` meant rivalry tracking silently stopped recording at
exactly the moment the rivalry became most serious. At `keep = 1` a government
staring at a finished foreign programme would have shed its rivalry with that
state at one month per three, and with it every gate that reads
`RivalryMonths`, including its own strategic preparation.

Enmity forms three times faster than it fades, which is the intended asymmetry.
Note the consequence of `keep = 1` at Standard difficulty: a state can only
accrue rivalry months in months where countering that rival is its single top
objective, so 24 months of rivalry takes considerably more than 24 months of
game time.

Anything that should depend on sustained hostility must read
`AIState.RivalryMonths`, not objective age. Tests:
`EndgameSystemTests.RivalryOutlivesTheObjectivesThatExpressIt` and
`RivalryFadesWhenThePressureComesOff`.

### 4b. Detected foreign programmes

`DetectedProgramme(state, observerId, targetId)` adds threat for a decisive
programme this government has **actually detected**. It walks every `EndgameType`
except `TotalMobilization`, reads
`EndgameSystem.KnownPreparation(observer, target, type)` — which returns −1 when
the observer cannot see it — and takes the worst of

```
alarm = knownProgress × (0.60 existential | 0.35 severe)
```

That figure goes into the `CounterRival` threat term **and, above 15, raises a
`PreemptProgramme` objective of its own** (§5b). Routing through
`KnownPreparation` is the point: an undetected programme frightens nobody, which
is exactly what makes concealment worth something (spec 14 §6). Knowing that
someone is close to being able to destroy you is not one input among many.

The weights set where the threshold bites. An existential programme clears 15 at
**25% progress**; a severe one needs **43%**. Nothing else in the world can
produce an alarm figure of this size, and at 100% progress an existential
programme scores `60 + 96 = 156`, which is roughly twice the top of any other
candidate's range. That is deliberate: a government that can see a finished
instrument aimed at it should have no other first priority.

## 5. Execution

`Act` walks the objective list in priority order until the budget is spent.

`BuildCapability` counts only a successful rebuilding action: logistics purchase,
war-footing change, asset order or procurement authorization. Non-Security priority,
unaffordable purchases, full programmes and no eligible weakened branch spend no
objective budget. The existing branch order and appetite draws are unchanged;
a failed logistics attempt still returns immediately. Dispatch may then reach
the next already-held objective, but does not retarget, replan or grant extra slots.
Earlier activity counts that treated every BuildCapability attempt as an action
overstated effective activity; those measurements need fresh native remeasurement.

### Domestic objectives

`ConsolidateHome` and `SecureResources` are **purchases**, not grants.
`ConsolidateHome` spends Political Capital through the same two verbs the player
uses; `SecureResources` spends treasury and cannot exceed the country's
endowment. Both can fail for want of the money, in which case the objective is
not counted against the month's action budget. Full treatment in §5a.

### Detection was dead, and the world fought instead (2026-09 suite repair)

`DetectedProgramme` reads `EndgameSystem.KnownPreparation`, and that returned −1
for every finished programme nobody was also collecting against: the disclosure
threshold was computed as `progress * 0.45f`, which evaluates to
44.999998807907104 and misses its own boundary of 45 by 1.2×10⁻⁶ (spec 14 §6).
So the alarm was always 0, `PreemptProgramme` was never raised for the case the
objective exists for, and the governments that should have been pre-empting spent
their action budget on `AssertClaim` instead.

**What it was driving, measured.** Six `WorldHeatTests` seeds, 360 months each,
across the original base, the disclosure fix alone, and the fix with the
eligibility correction below:

| across ~32,500 government-months | base | fix alone | eligibility | + reconsidered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `PreemptProgramme` selected | 8,829 | 26,251 | 16,411 | 8,923 |
| `CounterRival` | 5,425 | 1,186 | 7,283 | 11,313 |
| `HardenSecurity` | 3,346 | 5 | 3,604 | 6,638 |
| `BuildCapability` | 6,821 | 3,066 | 3,106 | 3,312 |
| `ExpandInfluence` | 3,372 | 921 | 939 | 961 |
| `ConsolidateHome` | 1,752 | 283 | 471 | 511 |
| `AssertClaim` | 1,521 | 132 | 132 | 138 |
| AI-vs-AI wars a government chose | 61 | 7 | 7 | 7 |
| satellite fronts | 64 | 43 | 38 | 43 |

**The fall in wars is not by itself evidence of a healthier world, and must not
be read as such.** With the disclosure fix alone the drop was mostly objective
starvation: a finished programme is visible for good and scores
`60 + alarm × 1.6`, so at Standard difficulty — where the action budget is one —
it took the only slot in 81% of all government-months, `AssertClaim` fell to
exactly zero in the second and third decade of every seed, and counter-espionage
stopped almost entirely.

### Pre-emption is raised only while it commands a response

`FormObjectives` raises `PreemptProgramme` only when
`AISystem.PreemptionResponseRemains` holds, and `PreemptProgramme` asks the same
question again before acting, so selection and execution cannot disagree about
what a response is. The predicate is read-only and derived entirely from state
the game already keeps — no alarm meter, no cooldown, no memory of what was
tried, and no change to the score:

- terms can be sought while we are actually fighting the holder;
- a deterrent of our own can be begun, while `EndgameSystem.CanPrepare` passes
  for some incomplete instrument;
- one of the ordinary tools is a *response* rather than routine watching: a
  station where there is none, hardening at home below 45, a legend, or coercion
  that is not already in force.

**Deepening an existing station is deliberately excluded, and that exclusion is
the correction.** Networks decay, so there is always another four points of
penetration to add; measured over two 20-year worlds that single step was why
pre-emption stayed eligible in 66% and 71% of the months it held the slot, while
counter-intelligence sat near 80. `CounterRival` still deepens collection under
its own objective, whose priority already carries this programme through
`threat`, so the threat is neither forgotten nor discounted — it competes
instead of pre-empting everything else in perpetuity. `CounterRival`'s last step
also stopped reporting itself as an action once penetration is capped at 100.

### An answered plan is reconsidered, not carried

Objectives are planned on a review cadence of `3 + patience/20` months, so a plan
is normally held for three to eight months before the government looks again.
That is deliberate — a government that re-plans every month reads as noise. It
has one exception, and the exception is not impatience:
`AISystem.HoldsAnAnsweredPreemption` reopens the planner when a pre-emption the
government is *still carrying* no longer satisfies `PreemptionResponseRemains`.

Without it, releasing the slot only half worked. `PreemptProgramme` correctly
declined an answered plan and charged nothing, but the objective stayed in the
list until the review came round and **nothing was planned in its place**, so the
freed slot sat empty. Measured over three seeds and 360 months: 22.4% of all
government-months at Standard, where the action budget is one, and 9.9% at
Challenging. Measured after: **zero at both**.

**It is keyed on eligibility, never on whether last month produced an action,
and that is the whole of the design.** A response can exist and still not fire —
`MountDeception` asks an appetite roll and `CounterRival`'s coercion step asks a
20% roll, both deliberately outside the eligibility predicate so that asking the
question cannot consume the month's randomness. Reconsidering after a quiet
month would re-plan after every failed roll and keep re-planning until one
succeeded, which is a reroll loop wearing a planner's clothes. An unlucky month
therefore leaves the plan exactly where it was; only a response that has
genuinely gone reopens the planner.

Every objective is examined rather than the first, because above Standard the
budget holds more than one and the answered plan can be the second of them.
Reconsideration grants no extra action budget, no retry and no second dispatch:
it re-runs the existing planner once, and the answered pre-emption cannot come
back from it because the candidate gate asks the same predicate. The planner
always offers `BuildCapability` as a fallback, so a government that has finished
answering a threat returns to ordinary business rather than standing idle — but
nothing is forced to succeed, and a month whose replacement objective cannot pay
for itself is still a quiet month.

**The execution-side check stays, and its job has changed.** With the planner
reconsidering, an answered plan should no longer reach dispatch at all, and the
census measures exactly zero such months. It is retained because what it defends
is the rule that selection and execution agree about what a response is: without
it, `PreemptProgramme` falls through to `CounterRival`, whose last step deepens a
station that almost always has another four points to give, so an answered plan
arriving by any route would spend the month's only action on routine collection
and report it as a pre-emption — roughly 6,000 government-months when the planner
still carried such plans. `ActionLadderTests` drives `AISystem.Act` directly to
pin that guarantee independently of what the planner happens to do.

**What the correction did and did not move.** Objective breadth returns:
counter-espionage from 5 back to 3,604 and then to 6,638 once answered plans
stopped holding the slot, `CounterRival` from 1,186 to 7,283 to 11,313, domestic
consolidation from 283 to 511, and pre-emption's share of the third decade from
about 95% to about 40% to about 28%. Actions taken per government-month rise
0.51 to 0.62 at Standard and 0.87 to 0.97 at Challenging — **an outcome, not a
target**: nothing here asks for a given action density. `AssertClaim` and the war count did **not**
move, and the reason is separate and unresolved: a finished instrument adds 60
to `threat` for as long as it exists, and by year 30 roughly 84% of
observer-target pairs can see one, so threat responses outrank territorial
claims almost everywhere. Whether a world in which nearly every state holds a
finished instrument *should* fight fewer wars is a design question, recorded in
`WorldHeatTests` and left open rather than tuned.

### `PreemptProgramme`

A rival is close to an instrument that ends us. Three responses in the order a
government would reach for them, all of them things the player can also do — §5b.

### `BuildCapability` → `InvestInPillars`, now a shell around `RebuildForces`

**This is no longer where a government's capability comes from.** It used to be: a
bespoke routine that raised pillars along the leader's national priority with no
people behind it, which meant a rival's progress had no explanation, nothing to
collect against, and nothing a coup could damage.

Every government now has a Cabinet, and `CabinetSystem.MonthlyAct` runs all
sixteen of them every month (spec 15 §1, §7). Pillar growth already happens
through the same five officials the player has, so **doing it here as well would
pay a government twice for one month's work**.

What remains is the part a cabinet does not cover:

```csharp
if (country.government.leader.priority == NationalPriority.Security)
    RebuildForces(state, country, rng);
```

Ministers raise a *pillar*; only a running procurement programme writes force
`strength`. `RebuildForces` still has to be reachable from somewhere, and this is
where it is reached from — which is also why it stays priority-locked to Security
(§9).

The objective still consumes an action from the month's budget whether or not
`RebuildForces` does anything, because `BuildCapability` is the only case in `Act`
that increments unconditionally rather than on a returned `true`.

### `RebuildForces` — the force, not just the headline number

Force `strength` is written upward in exactly one place in the whole simulation:
a running procurement programme. Procurement and logistics were **player-only
verbs**, so an AI army could be ground down by combat and by monthly sustainment
drag and never recover. A player could permanently disarm a rival by fighting
them once, the world quietly disarmed itself over a long save — and because
intelligence estimates read the *pillar* rather than the force, everyone's
reporting still said those states were strong.

Under a Security priority the AI now runs both verbs:

1. **Sustainment first.** If `logistics < 55`, a 50% monthly roll calls
   `MilitarySystem.InvestInLogisticsBy` (70 treasury, `logistics +9` through
   `Growth.Apply`) and stops there. A force without sustainment is hollow
   whatever its nominal strength.
2. Otherwise, if it already runs `MilitarySystem.MaxPrograms` (2), stop.
3. Find the branch with the **lowest** `strength`. If that is above 75 there is
   nothing worth a programme; stop.
4. Begin `MilitarySystem.BeginProcurementBy` at **Major** scale if the weakest
   branch is below 45, otherwise **Modest**.

| Scale | Months | Strength/month | Treasury/month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modest | 12 | 0.30 | 18 |
| Major | 24 | 0.40 | 34 |

`BeginProcurementBy` refuses when treasury is below three months of cost, so a
broke state cannot re-arm on paper. The AI never authorizes a **Transformative**
programme — that scale is gated behind a player skill.

Tests: `BugRegressionTests.AIStates_CanRebuildAForceTheyHaveLost` and
`AIStates_MaintainTheirLogistics`.

**Caveat:** `RebuildForces` is only reachable through `BuildCapability` under a
`Security` priority. A state whose leader wants prosperity or influence does not
maintain its army at all.

### `CounterRival` — a fixed ladder

`CounterRivalResponseRemains` is a read-only eligibility check shared by
candidate selection and dispatch. A capped station alone cannot hold a slot;
another response must remain. Every held CounterRival is checked before the
ordinary review interval, like an answered pre-emption, so exhausted targets
are reconsidered immediately, including below the leading slot. Ranking,
budgets and appetite probabilities are unchanged; filtering candidates and
earlier reviews can change later random draws and world trajectories.

Each successful step consumes one action and returns:

1. **Collect.** No network against the target → create one (`focus = Military`,
   `penetration = 15`). You cannot counter what you cannot see.
2. **Harden.** `counterIntelligence < 45` → +3.
3. **Deceive.** `MountDeception` (below).
4. **Coerce.** No existing sanction, `aggression > 45`, and the exposure is
   bearable — trade volume with them `< 55`, or `aggression > 70` and it does not
   care. The ordinary sanction cause must stand and no sanctions détente may
   be active. A 20% monthly roll then calls `EconomySystem.ImposeSanctionsBy` at
   `Coercive` severity if `aggression > 70`, else `Pressure`.
5. **Otherwise** deepen collection: `penetration +4`, capped at 100; at the cap
   this is not an action.

The deterministic coercion gates are shared with execution and pre-emption's
distinct-response check. Deception and coercion remain eligible when their
appetite roll fails: a quiet month is not exhaustion and must not trigger a
reroll. Decay or a changed world can make a previously capped target actionable
again. This does not redesign the fixed ladder or guarantee an action every
month. `ActionLadderTests` covers replacement, held-plan review, all response
branches, coercion refusals, read purity and failed-roll persistence. Balance
and long-run trajectory effects require separate measurement.

### `MountDeception` — the fog now runs both ways

Deception was a player-only verb, so the player could deceive everyone and be
deceived by nobody. A government that knows it is being watched and has a service
capable of building legends now mounts a programme of its own (GDD §14).

Gates, all required:

- **Intelligence pillar ≥ 45.** Legends take a real service.
- Own `deceptionStrength ≤ 40` — do not stack a programme already running.
- **The target must actually be collecting on us**: a network owned by them
  against us, with `penetration ≥ 20`. There is no point lying to someone who is
  not listening.
- A 25% monthly roll.

Direction follows from position, read through the fog like everything else:

```
weakerThanThem = ourMilitary < PerceivedStrength(them)
deceptionBias  = +1 (overstate) if weaker, −1 (understate) if stronger
deceptionStrength += 25 + opportunism × 0.15   (domain: Military)
```

The weak overstate to deter; the strong understate to invite a miscalculation
they can punish. Both read as reasonable from the inside, and both are exactly
the kind of understandable mistake the player should be able to make about them.

For comparison the player's `CovertOperation.Deception` adds a flat 30 for 2 CP
and is skill-gated. Both decay at 1.2/month in `IntelligenceSystem.MonthlyDecay`,
and both are bent through `GetEstimate` only when
`access < deceptionStrength × 0.6`, so strong collection still sees through it.

**A deception programme is a single global posture** — one domain, one bias per
country. Mounting one against a particular observer lies to *everyone* collecting
in that domain.

Test: `BugRegressionTests.TheFogRunsBothWays_AIStatesCanDeceiveUsToo`.

### `ExpandInfluence` → `SeekTreaty`

Proposes `NonAggression + TradePreference` through
`DiplomacySystem.ProposeTreatyBy`. On refusal it warms the relationship instead
(relations +1.5, trust +0.5) and tries again later — the month is still spent.

### `AssertClaim`

Refused outright if either party already has an active confrontation, or if
**domestic condition argues against foreign adventures**: `stability < 40` or
`warExhaustion > 55`. Then:

```
commitChance = 0.09 + aggression/300 + opportunism/400   (+0.05 Ruthless)
```

It targets the first non-capital location the target owns and opens with
`TerritorialConcession`, falling back to `Deterrence` when the target holds
nothing takeable. `PrimaryStrategy.Military` always. It never demands a capital —
that objective is unwinnable at the table (spec 01 §5).

## 5a. The domestic economy — every government has a budget

The foreign half of the AI had matched the player's verbs for a long time. The
domestic half had not. `ConsolidateHome` wrote `stability += 1.2`,
`approval += 0.8` and `government += 0.15`, and `SecureResources` wrote
`energy += 0.7` and `materials += 0.6`, every month, at no cost, through verbs the
player has no access to and cannot pay for. That is the same asymmetry this
project has already fixed once pointing the other way — AI states locked *out* of
player verbs (spec 12 §4b) — and it had two specific consequences:

1. **Domestic damage did not stick.** A rival could ride out any amount of
   destabilization, subversion or economic pain the player inflicted, because
   repairing it cost the rival exactly zero.
2. **The endowment model was quietly repealed.** `SecureResources` added energy
   and materials uncapped, and `EconomySystem`'s own comment states that a country
   can invest past its endowment "only through capability". An energy-poor
   archetype could stop being energy-poor by wanting to, and `energyDrag` stopped
   biting for anyone (spec 02 §5).

### `AIState.politicalCapital`

Every AI government now runs the player's Political Capital economy.

- **Stored on `AIState`, not `CountryState`**, so the player's
  `GameState.politicalCapital` and its save schema stay untouched. The field
  defaults to 0 and is filled on the first monthly tick, so no migration step is
  needed (spec 10).
- **One shared income formula**, `GovernmentSystem.PoliticalCapitalIncomeFor(country)`
  — spec 05 §2 has the terms. Approval, Government pillar, legislative support or
  elite cohesion, emergency powers and `CAP_CIVADMIN` all count, and the formula
  does not know who is running the government. A popular government with a
  cohesive elite is well funded politically whoever holds it, and a government
  that has spent its authority has to rebuild it the same way.
- **Operator skill is excluded from the shared formula.**
  `SkillEffect.PoliticalOperator` is added only to the player's pool, by the
  caller in `AccruePoliticalCapital`. Skills grant operator capability and never
  national power (spec 07), and putting the skill inside the shared formula would
  have handed the player's tree to fifteen foreign governments.
- **Both pools are capped at `GameState.PoliticalCapitalCap` = 20**, so a quiet
  AI government cannot bank a decade of authority and spend it all in one crisis.

Accessors: `PoliticalCapitalOf(state, countryId)` reads either pool;
`SpendPoliticalCapitalBy(state, countryId, amount, reason)` debits either and
returns false when the government cannot afford the act. It has to bite for an AI
exactly as it bites for the player, or the budget is decoration.

### `ConsolidateHome`

```
structural = governmentPillar < 55 or stability < 40
structural → InstitutionalReformBy   (6 PC)
otherwise  → PublicMessagingBy       (2 PC)
```

A government reaches for reform when the **machinery** is the problem and for
messaging when the **mood** is. Note the fall-through: if reform is the right
answer but the 6 PC is not there, the call fails and the government makes the case
to the public for 2 PC instead — which is what a government short of authority
actually does. Both verbs are `GovernmentSystem`'s actor-generic forms (spec 05
§2), so a foreign reform costs elite cohesion or legislative support exactly as
the player's does.

### `SecureResources`

```
InvestmentCost = 45 treasury
energyRoom     = EconomySystem.EnergyCeilingFor(state, country)  − energy
materialsRoom  = EconomySystem.MaterialsCeilingFor(country)      − materials

refuse if treasury < 45
refuse if energyRoom ≤ 0.5 and materialsRoom ≤ 0.5

energy    += min(0.7, energyRoom)     when energyRoom    > 0.5
materials += min(0.6, materialsRoom)  when materialsRoom > 0.5
```

The monthly increments are the same size they were when they were free; what has
changed is that they are bought, and that they can only close the gap toward what
the country actually has. Refusing when there is no room is the important half:
**a structural shortfall is not something money fixes**, and the answer to one is
a research programme, a trade partner or taking ground — exactly as it is for the
player (spec 02 §5). The two ceiling functions are public precisely so that
investment and the monthly drift share one definition.

### Governance versus intervention

The line this draws is **not** "nothing may move for free". Routine state
functioning is legitimately free on both sides, and it is now free through
**literally the same code**: an Autonomous Cabinet grants approval, stability and
pillar growth every month at no CP cost, and every government has one
(spec 15 §1). The AI no longer has an "equivalent" of the player's baseline — it
has the player's baseline. Asserting that nothing at all may move would forbid an
AI from having a working state apparatus while the player has one.

That is a stronger position than the one this section originally argued. When the
two sides ran different routines at comparable magnitude, the claim that they were
fair was a calibration judgement that could drift. It is now a structural
property.

What must never be free is **discretionary repair**: buying stability, approval,
unity, energy and materials on demand.
`AIDomesticTests.NoGovernmentBUYSDomesticRepairForFree` draws exactly that line —
it zeroes every treasury and every political-capital pool in the world, runs a
month of AI reasoning, and requires a bit-identical snapshot. Its `Snapshot`
helper covers `stability`, `governmentApproval`, `energy` and
`strategicMaterials`, and **deliberately excludes pillars and `nationalUnity`**
for the reason above. If a future change makes that snapshot move, something is
being granted rather than bought.

Other tests: `AIGovernmentsAccruePoliticalCapital`,
`PoliticalCapitalIncomeUsesOneFormulaForEveryone`, `PoliticalCapitalIsCapped`,
`ABankruptGovernmentCannotTalkItsWayOutOfTrouble`, `DomesticRepairSpendsTheBudget`,
`ReformCostsAForeignGovernmentItsGoodwillToo`,
`AResourcePoorStateStaysResourcePoor` (120 months of unlimited treasury must not
turn an energy-poor archetype into an energy-rich one), `SecuringResourcesCostsMoney`,
`ABrokeGovernmentCannotBuyResources`.

## 5b. Pre-emption

Until this existed, detection raised a threat number and changed nothing about
what the government did — the most consequential thing intelligence can report was
behaviourally inert (spec 14 §8, now closed).

**Scoring** (§4, §4b): raised for any state whose `DetectedProgramme` alarm
exceeds **15**, at `60 + alarm × 1.6`.

Two properties of that scoring are deliberate:

- **It is not discounted by caution.** Every other conclusion the AI draws from
  intelligence is reduced by `cautionPenalty` when the reporting is thin. This one
  is not, because it is the single conclusion a cautious government is *more*
  moved by, not less. A timid state does not shrug at an existential programme; it
  is the one most likely to act on a partial report of one.
- **It is not weighted by reach.** `AssertClaim` is multiplied by
  `GeographySystem.ReachFactorTo` because ambition is bounded by what a state can
  prosecute (§4). Alarm is not. A programme you cannot reach still ends you, and
  two of the three responses below — terms and a deterrent of your own — need no
  reach at all.

**Execution** (`PreemptProgramme`), in the order a government would reach for
them, and all three are things the player can also do:

1. **Stop being their target.** If we are in an active, unresolved confrontation
   with them, `ConfrontationSystem.ProposeSettlementBy` on our own authority. The
   cheapest counter to an instrument aimed at us is to stop being their problem,
   and a government staring at one will accept terms it would have refused a year
   earlier. Chronicled as seeking terms "in the shadow of a foreign programme".
2. **Build our own.** Walk every `EndgameType` except `TotalMobilization`, skip
   anything already at 100, and `EndgameSystem.PrepareBy` the first one
   `CanPrepare` allows. Deterrence is the classic answer and it is slow — which is
   exactly why detection has to happen early (spec 14 §2: roughly eight
   authorizations to reach 100).
3. **Fall back to `CounterRival`.** If neither is open, do what a threatened state
   does: collect, harden, deceive, coerce.

Note that step 2 is gated by the ordinary preparation gates — a 0.6-maturity
capability and a pillar ≥ 65 — so a state that cannot build an instrument goes
straight to step 3 rather than acquiring one out of fear.

#### Step 2 bypasses the 24-month rivalry gate, deliberately

This is the **second** route into strategic preparation. The first,
`ConsiderStrategicInstruments` (§6a), requires a rivalry at least **24 months**
old, so that states do not reach for the highest instruments over a passing
quarrel. `PreemptProgramme` does not consult that requirement at all.

An earlier pass flagged the omission as possibly unintended. It is intended, and
the code now carries a comment saying so:

- **A rival visibly close to an existential instrument is not a passing quarrel.**
  The 24-month gate exists to filter out transient hostility; a detected programme
  is the opposite of transient, and it is evidence about the *rival's* commitment
  rather than about the age of the quarrel.
- **Waiting two years to begin answering it defeats the point of detecting it
  early.** Preparation takes roughly eight authorizations; a government that must
  first accumulate two years of rivalry before it may start would finish its
  deterrent after the thing it was deterring had already been used. Detection
  would once again be behaviourally inert, which is the exact gap §5b was written
  to close.

**Every other gate still applies.** `CanPrepare` checks the capability, its
maturity, the pillar and the treasury, and `PrepareBy` charges per authorization.
What is skipped is the rivalry clock and nothing else, so a state that could not
otherwise build an instrument still cannot build one here.

Test: `AIDomesticTests.ADetectedProgrammeProducesDistinctBehaviour` completes a
`StrategicDestruction` programme in one state and requires an observer to hold a
`PreemptProgramme` objective, not merely a higher threat number. It uses a
*finished* programme on purpose: a completed programme crosses `KnownPreparation`'s
visibility threshold on its own signature with no collection at all (spec 14 §6),
so the test does not depend on the intelligence layer's state.

## 6. Running a confrontation

```
wantsOut = ourExhaustion > 45 + patience × 0.3
         or warSupport < 20
         or (momentum < −25 and caution > 50)
```

If it wants out and the confrontation is at least two months old it proposes
terms; if refused and exhaustion > 70 it may concede outright (25%/month).
Otherwise, below Limited Conflict it escalates one step
(`0.08 + aggression/500 + max(0,momentum)/400`, requires war support > 35); at or
above Limited Conflict it launches an operation on a 45% monthly roll, choosing
`Siege` when momentum is worse than −15 and `Assault` otherwise. Its directive is
derived from personality:

```
speedPriority     = 40 + aggression × 0.4
casualtyTolerance = 30 + aggression × 0.5
civilianRiskLimit = max(10, 60 − caution × 0.5)
```

so cautious governments are materially more restrained about collateral harm.

If the confrontation's objective location has already been taken it retargets the
opponent's first non-capital location.

**The AI settles wholesale, not by terms.** `ProposeSettlementBy` is the
pre-`PeaceSystem` path; AI governments never assemble a multi-term proposal
(spec 01 §5, "Negotiated terms").

## 6a. Strategic instruments (GDD §21)

`ConsiderStrategicInstruments` runs after confrontation management. Full detail in
[spec 14](14-Endgames.md); the AI-side rules are:

- **Prepare** against a rival of ≥ 24 months' standing (`LongStandingRival`, §4a)
  with treasury ≥ 400, at `0.18 + patience/400 + horizon×0.2` per month. It picks
  by `pillar + progress × 0.8`, so it finishes what it started rather than
  scattering effort.
- **Use** only from desperation — momentum < −30, exhaustion > 60, stability < 35,
  or having suffered an existential instrument from that opponent inside twelve
  months (+0.45 willingness). Base `0.10 + aggression/320 − caution/500`. It never
  reaches for one from a winning position.
- **Mobilize** at 25%/month once losing or in limited conflict or worse.

## 6b. Putting an occupation down (`ConsiderRelinquishment`) — C5

> **Reconstructed C5. Original acceptance verdict: FAIL.** The rule below is the
> measured one. Its known defect is that it reasons only about *cost* and never
> about *containment*: ground held against a state that has repeatedly attacked
> the holder (or the ally the holder entered to defend) is released on exactly
> the same terms as any other burden. Seed 1212's Turkey cycle is the clearest
> case. C5B owns that; it is deliberately absent here.

Runs monthly, immediately after `ManageOngoingConfrontation`, and is **outside the
objective budget** on the `ConsiderDetente` precedent: deciding you can no longer
afford a garrison is housekeeping a finance ministry does whether or not anything
else is happening, not a strategy competing for this month's actions. Routine
restocking spent thirty measured years unreachable behind a priority check and
this would have gone the same way.

**No RNG, no command points, no difficulty term.** A government that cannot pay
for an occupation cannot pay for it on Standard either.

```
if country.isPlayer                      -> return      (the operator decides)
bill = TerritorySystem.HoldingBillFor(country)
if bill <= 0                             -> return

if ConditionOf(country) <= CashNegativeButCreditworthy
   && treasury >= bill * HoldingRunwayMonths (36)
                                         -> keep everything
```

A solvent government keeps what it took. The exit exists because occupation was
*unpayable and inescapable*; a state that can plainly fund three years of garrison
has no reason to reach for it.

Otherwise each occupied location is considered on its own and must:

1. pass `TerritorySystem.CanRelinquish`;
2. be at least **`PostWarRelinquishmentMonths` = 24** past the relevant war
   ending — the same scale as the settlement truce. Immediately after a war a
   garrison is holding what the peace was argued over, and a government that
   walked out the following month would be settling twice. Months since a war
   ended is the established idiom, `MonthsSince(startDate) − monthsActive`, as
   `AllianceSystem.RecentlyAtWar` reads it. Never having fought reads as *long
   ago*: there is no war to wait out;
3. **not** be both uncontested *and* answering the holder's energy/materials
   shortfall. Either half failing — a rising on it, or no shortfall to answer —
   and it is just an expense.

**At most one withdrawal a month**, and it is the location with the largest
individual holding bill. A government withdrawing from everything at once reads
as a collapse rather than a decision, and staging it lets the next month's books
reflect what the last withdrawal saved.

## 7. Reading the player (GDD §24.2)

Roadmap #26: the separate actor-generic AIPrediction opponent model now counts
distinct partners from live sanctions plus public typed impositions within120
months inclusive. Removed measures age out at121 months; active ones still count.
Repeated targets do not stack. Existing tariff/embargo observations, intensity,
easing, confidence and budgets remain unchanged. coercionPrecedent persists a
dated public citation. This is an intentional post-lifting behavior change; no
inference from untyped legacy prose. Spec33 owns the record contract. The legacy
PlayerAssessment formula below remains live-state-only for sanctions.

Systemic pattern recognition over **observable** actions only:

```
aggressive  = player-initiated confrontations started within 120 months
            + treaties the player broke
coercion    = sanctions the player currently has in force
covert      = the player's networks that have been compromised
cooperative = unbroken treaties involving the player

pattern = aggressive × 12 + coercion × 8 + covert × 10 − cooperative × 6
perceivedAggression = clamp(pattern)
```

Above 40 it raises that country's threat perception of the player at 0.05/month
toward the assessment.

### The 120-month memory window

Counting **every confrontation ever opened** at full weight meant nine wars over
thirty years pinned the player at maximum perceived aggression for the rest of
the save, however they behaved afterwards. That removes the whole point of GDD
§24.2 — that a reputation can be established and then *broken*, which is what
makes deliberately establishing one a strategy rather than a scar.
`MemoryWindowMonths = 120` makes governments judge a pattern rather than keep a
permanent record. Test: `BugRegressionTests.AReputation_CanBeOutlived`.

The other three counts read live world state rather than history — a lifted
sanction and a lapsed treaty stop counting on their own — with one exception
noted in §9: a compromised network object is never removed, so exposed covert
action never fades.

Unexposed covert action is invisible to this entirely.

## 7a. Strategic paths — a theory of how to win (`Core/AIStrategy.cs`)

Before this, an AI government was a list of month-to-month reactions: stability
dipped so it consolidated, a rival looked strong so it countered. Averaged over a
decade that produces a state with no discernible ambition, which is a problem for
a game whose subject *is* ambition. A path makes a government legible — China
building for economic primacy behaves differently from China building for war,
and the player can work out which they face and play against it specifically.

`StrategicPath`: `EconomicPrimacy`, `MilitaryDominance`, `RegionalHegemony`,
`TechnologicalEdge`, `InstitutionalWeight`, `Survival`. Appended, never reordered.

**Chosen from endowments, not assigned.** `Suitability` scores each path from the
country's own pillars, resources and geography. The weakness penalty on
`MilitaryDominance` (`−max(0, 45 − military) × 1.4`) is load-bearing: without it
an aggressive personality was enough to make a minor power decide to dominate the
world, and wanting an army is not the same as having one.

**Reviewed rarely.** `MinimumMonthsOnPath = 30`, plus a `+10` inertia bonus for
staying the course. A government re-planning every month reads as noise, and
nothing the player learns about it stays true. Three things force an early
rethink: `pathProgress < FailingProgress (34)` sustained past a year, having just
lost a confrontation (`momentum < −20` within 30 months), and the first choice.

**Each cause buys one review, not one a month** (2026-09). The three triggers
used to be *conditions*, so once true they stayed true: past thirty months the
scheduled review fired every month, a failing path was reconsidered every month
of its second year, and a defeat stripped the inertia bonus for thirty months
running — measured on seed 4242, China flipped Survival ↔ TechnologicalEdge
eighteen months in a row at four to six wars with the two scores three points
apart, and the world made 44 strategy changes in a decade against a bar of 40.
`AIState.lastReviewMonthIndex` and `lastDefeatReviewedId` (additive, zero and
empty on old saves) make each a *once*: the scheduled review recurs every
`MinimumMonthsOnPath` since the last review, the failing review every
`FailingReviewMonths` (12) since the last, and a defeat is reviewed exactly once
(`RecentDefeat` returns the newest lost war's id; a second defeat is a second
review). `ReviewPath` still evaluates the same scores; only how often it is
asked changed. Tests: `ADefeatForcesOneRethink_NotOneEveryMonthOfTheShockWindow`,
`AFailingPathIsReconsideredOncePerYear_NotEveryMonth`.

**Progress is measured through the same fog everyone else uses.** `ProgressOn`
compares against `AISystem.PerceivedStrength` — *estimates* of rivals, not truth
— so a deceived state can believe it is winning. That is deliberate.

**`ObjectiveBias` is what stops a path being a label.** It shifts objective
priority directly: `EconomicPrimacy` puts `AssertClaim` at **−22** and
`SecureResources` at **+20**, so a trading power declines a war it could win.
`InstitutionalWeight` is at −26 on claims and +24 on influence. Test:
`AIStrategyTests.APathChangesWhatAGovernmentReachesFor`.

`AIState.disposition` is a hidden ±12 per-government tilt rolled once, so two
countries with similar endowments do not always reach the same conclusion. It is
*not* the main defence against a memorised opening — see §7b — it only stops
every game opening identically.

## 7b. Opponent modelling and counter-play (`Core/AIPrediction.cs`)

**The problem this solves.** A player must not be able to play a few months, work
out how the AI behaves, reset, and run a known-winning opening. Randomness is not
the answer — a world that behaves differently for no reason is worse than one
that behaves the same for good reasons. The answer is that **AI behaviour is a
function of what the player does**:

> An operator who always opens with covert action finds, by the third year, a
> world of hardened security services. One who always rushes militarily finds
> fortified neighbours with friends. Reset and repeat the opening and the same
> counter emerges — the world is not surprising you, it is *answering* you. The
> way past it is to change what you do.

`OpponentModel` per (observer, subject): `aggression`, `economicCoercion`,
`covertActivity`, `diplomaticActivity` (0..100 EWMA, `Adaptation = 0.09`),
plus `predictedPath`, `predictedMove`, `confidence`, `observations`.

**Rule 1 — only what could be seen.** Confrontations initiated (×3 weight if
against the observer), broken treaties, sanctions, embargoes and published
tariffs, treaties and coalitions, and covert action **that was caught**.
Everything here is a matter of public record.

**Rule 2 — a prediction must change a decision.** The old `PlayerAssessment` was
computed every month and read by exactly one line, so a player assessed as
maximally dangerous changed nothing about how the world behaved. That is
telemetry, not a prediction. `AddCounterObjectives` feeds three new objectives:

| Expectation | Objective | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| `Attack` | `HardenDefenses` | Alert posture, `missileDefense +3.5`, logistics, fortifies the weakest position held. 160 treasury + 1.2 PC |
| `Coerce` | `InsulateEconomy` | Spreads trade, confidence, energy toward the ceiling. 200 treasury |
| `Subvert` | `HardenSecurity` | `counterIntelligence +3.5`. 90 treasury + 0.8 PC |
| `Court` | `ExpandInfluence` | Answers a bloc by building one |

None of it is free — the same symmetry-of-consequence rule the rest of the AI is
held to. Test: `AIStrategyTests.PreparingIsNotFree`.

`Expectation()` returns 0 before `SettledAfter = 8` months of observation, and
scales by `0.35 + 0.65 × confidence`, so a government with no collection cannot
counter well. Caution adds hesitation (`caution × 0.18`) — a cautious state needs
more before it moves, exactly as it does elsewhere.

**Exposure is read from the chronicle, not from `network.compromised`.** That
flag is cleared again within a month or two as the network rebuilds; it answers
"is this blown right now", while a government forming expectations wants "have
they been caught doing this". The chronicle entry is now written for **every**
owner at `Publicity.Public` — it used to be player-only, so no AI state could
ever build a reputation for subversion.

**`InferMove` does not gate an expected attack on looking vulnerable.** A record
of coming for us specifically is its own prediction. Gating it meant a state that
grew its own army stopped expecting the wars it was already having, because the
fogged estimate of the aggressor stays roughly flat while one's own pillar climbs.

**It stays exploitable.** The EWMA decays when the behaviour stops, so a
reputation is a read on current conduct rather than a permanent sentence — which
matters because a permanent one would make reloading the correct response, and
this game refuses reloading. Test:
`AIStrategyTests.ThePlayerCanStillBreakTheirOwnPattern`.

## 7c. The world fights its own wars (GDD §24 amendment)

Census before the change: **0–1 AI-vs-AI wars in thirty years, across three
seeds** — while the same world carried 44–54 live AI-AI sanction regimes. The
economic instruments ran hot and the military ones never fired, because three
brakes stacked in `AssertClaim`:

- A flat "not while anyone involved is busy" guard — which made the
  overstretch opening (`IsOverstretched` feeding this exact objective's weight
  forty lines earlier) **structurally unreachable**: the busy are precisely the
  overstretched. Replaced with `ConfrontationSystem.CanOpenAnother` for the
  initiator — fronts priced, the same rule the player is held to — and no
  target-side block at all.
- A weakness-only candidate gate (`perceivedWeakness > 8`): two well-matched
  peer rivals could glare for thirty years and never collide. Deep rivalry
  (relations < 25) now also qualifies as cause.
- Nothing connected authored shortfalls to ground. **`ResourcePrize`**: an
  energy-poor state beside an `EnergyRegion` (or materials beside
  `MaterialsRegion`) gains commit chance (+0.10) and objective weight (+22),
  and the claim targets the prize location itself. The world-as-structure work
  finally reaches the trigger finger.
- Commit base 0.09 → 0.14; stability floor 40 → 35 (a world of standing
  sanctions keeps much of the roster in the high 30s, and the old bar quietly
  pacified all of it).

Post-change census: **~3 AI-vs-AI wars per 30 years** (2–6 by seed), player
drawn into 1–3, world relations ~15 points colder — the chosen "noticeably
hotter" calibration. `WorldHeatTests.TheWorldFightsItsOwnWars` holds the floor
and a ceiling. **Balance unmeasured since**; re-run `Report_MultiSeedBalance`.

**The fiscal reserve.** A hotter world's threat-inflated counter-spending
(HardenDefenses 160/act, spendable to zero) held every AI treasury at ~50
permanently — nothing treasury-gated could fire again: research stalled and no
government prepared a strategic instrument in twenty years despite nine
decade-long rivalries (measured gate-by-gate on seed 90210).
`DiscretionaryReserve = 250` on the three counter-verbs — a government does not
spend its last coin on this month's fear — and the endgame pre-check derives
from `PreparationTreasuryCost × 1.5` rather than a hand-written 400 (3× the
real gate; a second definition of affordability is a repeal of the first).
After: two governments preparing by year 14, world matured capabilities
doubled. The general lesson, now three times over in one tranche: **a busier
AI must not stop doing its other jobs** — attention (war footing), policy
(HardenSecurity priority), and money (the reserve) all need explicit
protection from the urgent crowding out the important.

## 7d. Estimates, not truth — the point-reads removed (core stability repair, 2026-09)

Seven AI decisions about *other* countries read those countries' hidden state
directly. Each now goes through the perception layer, and `AIInformationTests`
holds each to two properties: changing the hidden truth without changing what
the AI has been told does not change the decision, and changing what it has been
told does.

| Site | Read | Now |
|---|---|---|
| `TreatyOfferFor` | target's true military and intelligence pillars | `PerceivedStrength(Military)`; intelligence-sharing offered when *we* can see (our own pillar) and they feel threatened |
| `AIStrategy.NeighbourhoodDominance` | every neighbour's true military pillar | `PerceivedStrength(Military)` — this decided which path a government adopted and whether it judged the path failing |
| `AIPrediction.InferPath` | target's true `stability` | `PerceivedStrength(Political)` |
| `ChooseOperation` | target location's true `garrison` | `PerceivedGarrison`: the midpoint of `TryEstimateGarrison`'s band (deception-bent, as the player's), or a flat prior of 50 with no reporting; fortifications stay public — works are visible from orbit, a headcount is not |
| `MountDeception` / `DeceptionDomainFor` | the rival's own network record (penetration, focus) | `SuspectsCollection`: a network we have caught, or a strong service (≥ 55) assuming it of a cold state; a caught network reveals its focus, a suspected one does not, so we protect our strongest pillar |
| `InsurgencySystem.ConsiderSponsorship` | a rising's exact support and strength | `PerceivedViability`: unseen (−1) with no Political reporting on the holder, quantised to 25/20/10/5-wide bins by grade, exact only for a movement we already arm |
| `ConsiderDetente` | the sender's `ReliefWillingness` | `EconomySystem.AssessRelief` (spec 02 §9a): skips a sender only when *reporting* reads it as unlikely; with none it asks and may be refused |

Left as legitimate: the AI's own state everywhere; public facts (treaties,
sanctions, confrontations, ownership, the public chronicle); the council's whip
count (chamber politics are semi-public — noted, not changed); treaty
acceptance reading a proposer's capability *effects* (broadcasting, aid,
inspection regimes are things the deciding side experiences); the no-collection
fallback `40 + marketIndex × 0.1` (a published figure).

## 8. Extension points

- **Cross-pillar coordination.** Objectives execute independently. A genuinely
  coordinated AI would sequence them (collect → isolate diplomatically → coerce
  economically → assert claim). Planning horizon is the natural lever.
- ~~**Offensive coalitions.**~~ **Closed 2026-08-27.** `DiplomacySystem.RequestCoalitionBy`
  is actor-generic and `AISystem.ConsiderCoalition` reaches for it once a war has
  reached Limited Conflict (chance by opportunism/aggression, diplomacy pillar ≥ 40).
  Coalitions went from 12 of 556 measured decades to a routine feature. In the same
  pass `AISystem.ConsiderResearch` added instrument-directed research (the existing
  `TechnologySystem.ConsiderAiResearch` only followed national priority at 6%/month and
  was starved by the pre-fix treasury), which is what makes their strategic instruments buildable (spec 14).
- **Objective hysteresis.** With `keep = 1` at Standard difficulty an AI can
  thrash between objectives when scores are close. A bonus for the incumbent
  objective would help, and would also make `RivalryMonths` accumulate more
  smoothly.
- **Doctrine/ideology** — GDD §24.1 lists ideology as an input; today only
  personality and national priority shape behavior.

## 9. Open questions / PLANNED

Honest inventory of what the AI is *not*:

- **It has a Cabinet, but no institution around it.** Every government now runs
  five officials and grows its pillars through them (spec 15 §1, §7). What an AI
  state still lacks is the operator layer: no control modes, no Influence, no
  `AuthoritySystem` (spec 05 §1a), and no reporting filter — it reasons straight
  from its own estimates with nothing between the world and the decision. A
  foreign cabinet is permanently `Autonomous`, because there is nobody standing
  outside it to direct or override anyone. An AI government's structural type
  still changes its succession and its coup arithmetic and nothing about how it
  acts abroad.
- **It has the half of the resource economy that matters.** It runs Political
  Capital on the same income formula and the same cap as the player (§5a), and
  pays treasury where a verb charges it. It has **no Command Points and no
  Influence**, and this is settled rather than outstanding: CP is the operator's
  attention budget rather than a national resource, and Influence exists only to
  buy a directive from an official who would otherwise choose for themselves —
  a verb no foreign cabinet has, because no operator stands outside it. Breadth
  of action is governed by the per-month action count instead.
- **No Crisis Turns, by decision.** A Crisis Turn exists to interrupt the
  *player's* month and force a decision at the terminal. An AI government simply
  decides. There is nothing to interrupt.

**Neither of the two above is missing work** (user decision, 2026-08-21). They
are operator *interfaces*, not government machinery, and building them would add
simulation the player can never see in exchange for no behaviour they could ever
detect. The fairness rule this spec is built on is symmetry of **consequence** —
nothing free for one side and paid for by the other — which §5a satisfies.
Symmetry of *interface* was never the requirement.
- **Verbs it never uses:** joint exercises (so interoperability blocs never form
  organically between AI states), covert operations proper (it creates networks
  and raises counterintelligence directly instead of running
  `CovertOperation`), technology programmes (`TechnologySystem` is
  player-driven, so capability diffusion into the AI world happens only through
  treaties, exercises and espionage against the player), and multi-term peace
  proposals.
- **`RebuildForces` is priority-locked.** Only a Security-priority leader
  maintains the force at all (§5).
- **Pre-emption has one shape.** `PreemptProgramme` (§5b) closed the inert-detection
  gap, but its three responses are terms, a deterrent, and the ordinary
  `CounterRival` ladder. A government eight months from being destroyed might also
  reasonably strike the programme itself, buy the sponsor off, or trade a
  concession elsewhere for its cancellation — none of which exist as verbs.
- **Exposed covert action never fades from the player assessment**, because a
  compromised `IntelNetwork` is never removed from `state.networks`. The 120-month
  window covers confrontations only.
